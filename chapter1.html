Data Distribution & Parametric Model

Let $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ denote an unknown probability distribution on $\mathbb{R}^d$ from which we observe a finite collection of independent samples $\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N\}$. The central problem in generative modeling is to construct a parametric model $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ that approximates $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ and enables efficient sample generation.

Definition: Data Distribution
Let $\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be i.i.d. samples from a probability density $p_{\mathrm{data}}:\mathbb{R}^d\to[0,\infty)$. Let $K\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ be a compact set containing the main mass of $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. We assume $$ p_{\mathrm{data}}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(K)\cap\mathcal{C}^{2}(K), \qquad p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})\to 0 \;\text{ as }\; \|\mathbf{x}\|\to\infty. $$ We observe only the empirical collection $$ \mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_N \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} p_{\mathrm{data}}. $$
Definition: Parametric Model
Approximate $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ by a model specified via an unnormalized density $q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\ge 0$: $$ p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}{Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})}, \qquad Z(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}, $$ with $\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^m$. The log-likelihood decomposes as $$ \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \log q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) \;-\; \log Z(\boldsymbol{\theta}). $$

Kullback–Leibler Divergence

A natural measure of discrepancy between two probability densities is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Intuitively, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ quantifies the expected amount of information (measured in nats) lost when $q$ is used to approximate the true distribution $p$. It arises naturally from the principle of maximum likelihood: the parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ is exactly the one that maximizes the log-likelihood of the data under $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$.

Definition: Kullback–Leibler Divergence
Let $p$ and $q$ be probability densities on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $p\ll q$. The Kullback–Leibler divergence from $q$ to $p$ is defined as $$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p}\!\left[ \log p(\mathbf{x}) - \log q(\mathbf{x}) \right]. $$ It is non-negative and vanishes if and only if $p = q$ almost everywhere.

Connection to Maximum Likelihood

Since $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})]$ does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, minimizing the KL divergence reduces to maximizing the expected log-likelihood:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = \underbrace{ \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}] }_{\text{constant w.r.t.}\;\boldsymbol{\theta}} -\; \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}} \;\equiv\; \max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]. \]

Universal Approximation for Densities

Theorem — UAT for Densities For any continuous density $p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ and any $\epsilon>0$, there exists a neural-network-based parametric model $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ such that $$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigr)<\epsilon. $$

The proof relies on the Universal Approximation Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1989): neural networks with a single hidden layer and sigmoidal activations uniformly approximate any continuous function on compact sets. Let $K\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ contain the main mass of $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. For $f(\mathbf{x})=\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ and any $\epsilon_1>0$, there exists a network $\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ with

\[ \left|\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\right| < \epsilon_1, \qquad\forall\,\mathbf{x}\in K. \]

We construct the parametric model via the unnormalized energy $\exp(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}))$:

\[ p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}))}{Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})}, \qquad Z(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int_K\!\exp(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}))\,d\mathbf{x}. \]

The next step is to bound $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ in terms of $\epsilon_1$, which requires controlling both the approximation error and the normalization constant $Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.

Using the decomposition $\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-\log Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, the KL divergence splits into two controllable terms:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = \underbrace{ \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}} \!\left[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\right] }_{\text{Term I}} + \underbrace{ \log Z(\boldsymbol{\theta}) }_{\text{Term II}}. \]

Bounding Term I

By Jensen's inequality and the uniform approximation,

\[ \left| \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}} \!\left[\log p_{\mathrm{data}}-\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right] \right| \le \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}} \!\left[ \left|\log p_{\mathrm{data}}-\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right| \right] < \epsilon_1 \int_K p_{\mathrm{data}}\,d\mathbf{x} \approx \epsilon_1. \]

Bounding Term II

From $|\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-\log p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})|<\epsilon_1$,

\[ e^{-\epsilon_1} \frac{\exp(\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}))}{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})} e^{\epsilon_1}. \]

Integrating against $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ and using $\int_K p_{\mathrm{data}}\approx 1$ yields $e^{-\epsilon_1}< Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})< e^{\epsilon_1}$, hence $|\log Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})|<\epsilon_1$.

Combining both estimates, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) < \epsilon_1+\epsilon_1 = 2\epsilon_1$. Choosing $\epsilon_1=\epsilon/2$ completes the proof. $\quad\square$

Evaluating $Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\int q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is intractable in high dimensions. Hyvärinen (2005) proposed score matching: minimize the squared distance between score functions, bypassing $Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ entirely.

Normalization Problem & Score Function

Definition: Score Function The score of $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is the gradient of its log-density: $$ \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}). $$ Since $\log Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is constant in $\mathbf{x}$, $$ \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) \;\underbrace{-\;\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{=\;0} \;=\; \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}). $$ The score is independent of $Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.
Geometric Interpretation The score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{data}}$ defines a vector field on $\mathbb{R}^d$ pointing toward regions of higher probability, independent of global scaling.

When data lies on a submanifold $\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^d$, the density is degenerate off $\mathcal{M}$ and the score is undefined or explosive near it. Adding noise perturbations expands the support to all of $\mathbb{R}^d$, yielding a well-behaved score field everywhere.

Fisher Divergence & Explicit Score Matching

Since the KL divergence is intractable for unnormalized models, we need an alternative that compares scores rather than densities. Because the score is invariant to multiplicative constants, minimizing the Fisher divergence yields a feasible surrogate for maximum likelihood.

Definition: Fisher Divergence For two sufficiently smooth densities $p$ and $q$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$, $$ D_F(p\|q) = \frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p(\mathbf{x}) \big\| \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x}) - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q(\mathbf{x}) \big\|^2 d\mathbf{x}. $$
Explicit Score Matching Objective For a target density $p$, define $$ E(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) \;\triangleq\; \frac{1}{2}\, \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p} \!\Big[ \big\| \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x}) \big\|_2^2 \Big]. $$ When $p=p_{\mathrm{data}}$, this equals $D_F(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$. However, it requires the unknown true score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{data}}$ making it uncomputable in practice.

The key insight of Hyvärinen (2005): integration by parts eliminates the unknown score, yielding an equivalent objective that depends only on the model score and its divergence.

Implicit Score Matching

Proposition — Implicit Score Matching Assume $p$ is a smooth density on $\mathbb{R}^d$ such that $p(\mathbf{x})\to0$ and $\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|\to0$ as $\|\mathbf{x}\|\to\infty$. Then $$ E(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) = I(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) + C, $$ where $C$ is a constant independent of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and $$ I(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p} \!\Big[ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|_2^2 \Big]. $$

Expanding the squared norm in $E(\boldsymbol{\theta};p)$:

\[ E(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_2^2\bigr] \;-\; \underbrace{ \mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p\bigr] }_{\text{cross term}} + \underbrace{ \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p\|_2^2\bigr] }_{=\ C,\;\text{independent of }\boldsymbol{\theta}}. \]

For the cross term, rewrite $\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p = \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p / p$, so

\[ \mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p\bigr] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}. \]

Integrating by parts and using the decay hypotheses (boundary terms vanish):

\[ \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} p\,d\mathbf{x} = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p(\mathbf{x})\, \bigl(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr) \,d\mathbf{x} = -\mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigr]. \]

Substituting the integration-by-parts result back into the expansion of $E(\boldsymbol{\theta};p)$:

\[ E(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) = \mathbb{E}_p \!\Big[ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|_2^2 \Big] + C = I(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p) + C. \quad\square \]

The objective $I(\boldsymbol{\theta};p)$ is now computable from data without ever evaluating $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{data}}$. Its two terms play complementary roles:

Energy term $$ \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|_2^2\bigr] $$ Penalizes artificially steep gradients: forces the estimated score field to have bounded magnitude, preventing the model from collapsing to a degenerate point mass.
Curvature term $$ \mathbb{E}_p\bigl[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr] $$ Forces the divergence of the estimated field to match the local geometry of the log-density: regions where probability mass concentrates must act as attractors (negative divergence).

Practical Limitations of Score Matching

Despite its theoretical foundation, direct score matching encounters two fundamental obstacles that prevent its use in practice.

Problem 1: Computational Cost

Evaluating $I(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p)$ requires computing

$$ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \frac{\partial [\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]_i}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{x}) $$

which costs $O(d)$ backward passes, prohibitive for large $d$. Sliced Score Matching (Song et al., 2020) reduces this via random projections, but a more fundamental limitation remains.

Problem 2: Low-Density Regions

The expectation in $I(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,p_{\mathrm{data}})$ is taken with respect to $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. Consequently, low-probability regions contribute negligibly to the loss regardless of how wrong $\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is there.


The model receives no informative gradients where data are sparse. For generative sampling via Langevin dynamics, a reliable score estimate is needed everywhere, not just near the modes.

Denoising Score Matching — Setup

Both problems disappear when we work with a perturbed version of the data. Adding noise $\mathbf{z}$ to $\mathbf{x}$ produces a smoothed distribution with full support, eliminating low-density regions and whose score can be approximated by a tractable conditional that converges to the true score as $\sigma\to 0$.

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a complete probability space with:

  • $\mathbf{x}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^d$ — clean data with density $p_{\text{data}}$ w.r.t. $\lambda^d$.
  • $\mathbf{z}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^d$ — additive noise with density $p_{\mathbf{z}}$ w.r.t. $\lambda^d$.
  • Independence hypothesis: $\mathbf{x}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{z}$, i.e. for all $A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)$, $\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\in A,\,\mathbf{z}\in B)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\in A)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{z}\in B)$.

The noisy observation is $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}$. The perturbed distribution $q_\sigma$ is the marginal of the joint $(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x})$, obtained by integrating out $\mathbf{x}$:

\[ q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\,p_{\mathbf{z}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}\bigl[p_{\mathbf{z}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})\bigr]. \]

When $p_{\mathbf{z}}$ has full support, $q_\sigma$ inherits it, the score $\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma$ is well-defined everywhere and informative gradients are guaranteed throughout $\mathbb{R}^d$.

Conditional Distribution under Additive Noise

Lemma — Conditional Distribution Under the independence hypothesis, the conditional density of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ given $\mathbf{x}$ is $$ q(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) = p_{\mathbf{z}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}), \qquad \lambda^{2d}\text{-a.e. } (\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}. $$

Proof. Since $\mathbf{x}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{z}$, the joint density factorizes as $f_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}(\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{z}_0)=p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}_0)\,p_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{z}_0)$. Define the bijection $T(\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{z}_0)=(\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{x}_0+\mathbf{z}_0)$ with inverse $T^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_0,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0)=(\mathbf{x}_0,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0-\mathbf{x}_0)$. The Jacobian of $T^{-1}$ is

\[ J_{T^{-1}} = \begin{pmatrix} I_d & 0 \\ -I_d & I_d \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(J_{T^{-1}}) = 1, \]

so $T^{-1}$ preserves volume. By the change-of-variables theorem,

\[ f_{(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\mathbf{x}_0,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0) = p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}_0)\,p_{\mathbf{z}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0-\mathbf{x}_0). \]

Dividing by the marginal $p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}_0)$ yields $q(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_0)=p_{\mathbf{z}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0-\mathbf{x}_0)$, valid for $\lambda^d$-a.e. $\mathbf{x}_0$. $\square$
The conditional is simply the noise density evaluated at the residual $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}$, regardless of $p_{\text{data}}$.

Gaussian Noise — Conditional Score

The lemma is fully general. For Gaussian noise $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2 I_d)$ the conditional takes an explicit form and its score admits a closed-form expression, the analytical engine of DSM.

Corollary — Gaussian Noise If $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2 I_d)$, then $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}};\,\mathbf{x},\sigma^2 I_d)$ and the conditional score satisfies $$ \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}}{\sigma^2}. $$

Proof. By the lemma, $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathbf{z}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})$. Substituting the Gaussian density $p_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{z})=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}\exp(-\|\mathbf{z}\|^2/2\sigma^2)$ at $\mathbf{z}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}$ gives $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x},\sigma^2 I_d)$. Taking the logarithm, $\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})=\text{const}-\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|^2/2\sigma^2$. Differentiating component-wise in $\tilde{x}_i$ yields $-(\tilde{x}_i-x_i)/\sigma^2$, and collecting all components gives the result. $\square$

The analytical tractability of this expression is the cornerstone of DSM: it provides a closed-form target that does not require knowledge of $p_{\text{data}}$, transforming density estimation into a supervised denoising regression task. For a general noise distribution, \(\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) =\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\log p_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{u})\big|_{\mathbf{u}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}}\).

ESM and DSM Objectives on $q_\sigma$

We now formalize the two objectives on the perturbed distribution $q_\sigma$ and establish their equivalence.

Explicit Score Matching (ESM) $$ E(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,q_\sigma) \triangleq \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_\sigma} \Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) - \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr] $$

Targets the score of the marginal $q_\sigma$. Evaluating $\nabla\log q_\sigma$ requires integrating over $p_{\text{data}}$; intractable.

Denoising Score Matching (DSM) $$ D(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,q_\sigma) \triangleq \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}} \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})} \Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) - \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr] $$

Replaces the marginal score with the conditional score $-(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$; tractable in closed form.

The central question, is minimizing $D$ with the closed-form conditional score equivalent to minimizing the intractable $E$?, is answered affirmatively up to an additive constant.

Theorem — Equivalence ESM-DSM (Vincent, 2011) For any $\sigma > 0$ and any measurable $\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ with $\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}\bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|_2^2\bigr]<\infty$, there exists a constant $C$ independent of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ such that $$E(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,q_\sigma) = D(\boldsymbol{\theta};\,q_\sigma) + C.$$

Proof

Apply Proposition (ISM decomposition) to $p=q_\sigma$: there exists $C_1$ independent of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ such that $E(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma)=I(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma)+C_1$, where

\[ I(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}\!\Big[ \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|_2^2 \Big]. \]

Expanding $I$ and $D$ in their squared-norm form and collecting terms:

\[ I(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma) = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}\bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|_2^2\bigr] + \mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}\bigl[\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\bigr], \]

\[ D(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma) = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})} \bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|_2^2\bigr] - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})} \bigl[\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\bigr] + C_2. \]

Step 1 — Energy terms coincide. Since $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\int p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\,q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$, the law of total expectation gives directly

\[ \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})} \bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|_2^2\bigr] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}\bigl[\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\|_2^2\bigr]. \]

It remains to prove the equality of the cross terms.

Proof

Step 2 — Cross terms via marginal representation and Fubini. Using $q_\sigma$ as a marginal and substituting its integral representation, exchanging gradient with integral (justified by the integrability condition):

\[ \mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}\bigl[ \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) \bigr] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot \Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^d}p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\, \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\Bigr)d\tilde{\mathbf{x}}. \]

Writing \(\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) =q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\) and applying Fubini yields \(\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})} [\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})]\).

Step 3 — Integration by parts of the curvature term. Integrating \(\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}[\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})]\) by parts gives exactly \(-\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma}[\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})]\), which by Step 2 equals \(-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})} [\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})]\). Substituting Steps 1–3:

\[I(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma)+C_1 = D(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma)+(C_1+C_2) \;\Longrightarrow\; E(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma) = D(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma)+C,\quad C=C_1+C_2.\quad\square\]

Interpretation Minimizing $D(\boldsymbol{\theta};q_\sigma)$ with the tractable conditional score $-(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$ is exactly equivalent to minimizing the intractable ESM objective. The analogy: ESM directly senses the marginal score at your location and DSM, displaced by the same noise, infers the direction from the observed residual $-(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$, converging to the same field without ever measuring the marginal score.

Noise-Conditioned Score Networks

The ESM–DSM equivalence provides a practical path: instead of the intractable marginal score $\nabla\log q_\sigma$, we train a score network $\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},\sigma)$ to match the closed-form conditional score $-(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$. For a single noise level $\sigma$, this yields the denoising objective:

Single-Scale Denoising Objective $$ \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\sigma) = \frac{1}{2}\, \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x})}\, \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x},\sigma^2 I)} \!\Big[ \Big\| \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\sigma) + \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}}{\sigma^2} \Big\|_2^2 \Big]. $$

A single $\sigma$ is not enough: a large $\sigma$ provides training signal in sparse regions but blurs fine structure; a small $\sigma$ preserves detail but leaves low-density regions without informative gradients. The solution is a geometric progression of noise levels $\sigma_1>\sigma_2>\cdots>\sigma_L>0$, where:

Large Guarantees a non-zero training signal even in the sparsest regions of $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. The perturbed distribution $q_{\sigma_1}$ has full, diffuse support score estimates are reliable everywhere.
Small Ensures $q_{\sigma_L}\approx p_{\mathrm{data}}$: at the finest scale the score network approximates the true data score, enabling accurate sample generation by Langevin dynamics.

Multi-Scale NCSN Objective

Following Song & Ermon (2019), the multi-scale training objective is a weighted average over all noise levels:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \lambda(\sigma_i)\,\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\sigma_i), \qquad \lambda(\sigma)>0. \]

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ drives $\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\sigma_i)$ toward the true score $\nabla\log q_{\sigma_i}$ for each scale. The critical design choice is the weighting $\lambda(\sigma)$.

Weighting
Near optimality, $\|\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},\sigma)\|_2\propto 1/\sigma$. The weighted loss becomes $$ \sigma^2\,\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\sigma) = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E} \!\Big[ \Big\| \sigma\,\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\sigma) + \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}}{\sigma} \Big\|_2^2 \Big]. $$ Since $(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I)$, both terms inside the norm remain $O(1)$ regardless of $\sigma$, balancing gradient magnitudes across all scales.
Final NCSN Training Loss $$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCSN}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \sigma_i^2\, \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{data}}}\, \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x},\sigma_i^2 I)} \!\Big[ \Big\| \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\sigma_i) + \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}}{\sigma_i^2} \Big\|_2^2 \Big]. $$ This objective sidesteps both limitations of classical score matching: $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is never computed, and the multi-scale noise injects informative gradients everywhere, even where $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ is negligible.

Empirical Validation — Experiment Setup

We instantiate the NCSN training objective on a controlled two-dimensional example. The goal is twofold: to empirically validate the ESM–DSM equivalence and to demonstrate that the learned score network provides accurate estimates even in low-density regions.

Bimodal Target Distribution $$ p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{x}) = 0.2\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_1,I) + 0.8\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_2,I) $$ with $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1=(-5,-5)$ and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_2=(5,5)$. The two modes are well separated, leaving a low-density valley that challenges score estimation where data are scarce.

The loss minimized is the DSM objective with $\lambda(\sigma)=\sigma^2$, where $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I)$:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L} \sigma_i^2\, \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}} \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I)} \!\Big[ \Big\| \mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}+\sigma_i\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,\sigma_i) + \frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{\sigma_i} \Big\|_2^2 \Big]. \]

Results — Sample Generation

To validate the learned score, new samples are generated via annealed Langevin dynamics. Starting from pure noise, particles iterate $\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_t+\alpha\,\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,\sigma_t)+\sqrt{2\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ with $\sigma_t$ decreasing from $\sigma_{\max}=4.0$ to $\sigma_{\min}=0.001$.

Conclusion Both modes and their relative weights are accurately recovered. The empirical success corroborates all theoretical guarantees established in the preceding slides: the multi-scale DSM objective provides informative gradients everywhere, and annealed Langevin dynamics converges to $p_{\mathrm{data}}$ from pure noise.
